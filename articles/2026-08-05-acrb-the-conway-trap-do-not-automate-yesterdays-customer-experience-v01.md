# The Conway Trap: Do Not Automate Yesterday's Customer Experience

**Deck:** Retailers do not face a simple race to deploy more AI; they face a harder test of whether their systems, incentives, and decision rights can produce one coherent customer experience rather than seven optimized fragments.

## The fast way to stand still

Retail bosses are being told to move fast with AI. Many are. A service copilot here, a pricing engine there, a merchandising assistant elsewhere. The activity looks impressive. The risk is that it changes less than it seems.

The strongest evidence does not show that retail has already entered an age of autonomous machine commerce. It does show something more immediate and, for incumbents, more awkward. A synthesis of digital transformation and organizational mirroring research suggests that when companies digitize inside existing silos, they often reproduce those silos in software, data flows, incentives, and customer journeys.[1][2][3][4][5]

That matters because customers do not shop by department. They do not care that discovery sits in marketing, availability in supply chain, checkout in e-commerce, and returns in store operations. They experience one retailer, not a federation of internal fiefdoms.[6][7] Omnichannel research has made this point for years. Customers expect continuity across channels, consistent promotions, reliable inventory visibility, and service that remembers what just happened.[6][8] AI raises the stakes because it increases the speed, scale, and visibility of decisions. AI can make inconsistency more visible by increasing the speed and scale of customer-facing decisions.[7][9]

This is the Conway trap. Retailers may use AI to accelerate yesterday's operating model just as customer expectations are shifting again. The danger is not that a marketing team deploys an agent, or that a contact center automates routine work. Those can be sensible first steps. The danger is mistaking a portfolio of departmental pilots for transformation.[1][2][10]

## The second org chart

The internal mechanism is easy to miss because it often arrives disguised as progress. Each function acquires its own AI tools, its own prompts, its own data pipelines, its own success metrics. Merchandising gets a copilot for assortment and content. Service gets one for triage and handle time. Marketing gets one for targeting and creative variation. Operations gets one for forecasting and fulfillment support.

Taken one by one, these tools can work. Evidence from generative AI in customer support and knowledge work suggests that assistive systems can improve productivity, especially when they are embedded in workflows and used with human oversight.[11][12] The problem begins when each success is measured locally and governed locally.

What emerges is a second org chart: a machine layer that reflects the same boundaries as the human one. If separate functions commission, govern, and tune their own systems, the machine organization will inherit the same fragmented objectives and incomplete context.[4][5][13]

The result is a peculiar form of modernization. The retailer becomes faster at doing disconnected things. A pricing agent optimizes margin. A marketing agent optimizes click-through. A service agent optimizes speed. Each may improve its own metric while the customer gets contradictory offers, unavailable recommendations, or a post-purchase experience that seems to have forgotten the pre-purchase promise.[7][10] Local efficiency rises. End-to-end performance does not necessarily follow.

This is not a theoretical quibble. Research on digital transformation has long found that technology investments disappoint when firms digitize existing processes without redesigning workflows, roles, and incentives.[1][2][14] Studies of general-purpose technologies suggest that returns are delayed because complementary organizational changes are required.[15] AI appears to fit that pattern.[11][12]

## Customers are already ahead of the org chart

The case for redesign does not rest on visions of consumers delegating all purchases to autonomous agents. Evidence for that remains thin and uneven. The more immediate pressure comes from a simpler fact: customers already judge retailers across journeys rather than touchpoints, and digital interfaces are making them less tolerant of friction.[7][8]

This is especially clear in retail categories where the journey is inherently interdependent. In fashion, discovery, fit confidence, availability, fulfillment, returns, and loyalty are tightly linked. In luxury, service tone, clienteling, and brand control are part of the product, not an optional wrapper. A retailer that offers elegant AI-assisted discovery but clumsy post-purchase service has not created a premium experience. It has merely made the inconsistency easier to see.

There is evidence, too, that consumers increasingly expect convenience, continuity, and relevance across channels.[6][8] Separately, AI-assisted tools are beginning to influence how some shoppers discover and evaluate products online.[16] Company launches such as Amazon's Rufus show that conversational shopping assistance is no longer hypothetical.[17] Yet most of these systems remain bounded assistants, not autonomous buyers roaming the market on a customer's behalf.[17] The prudent conclusion is not that tomorrow's customer has fully arrived. It is that the transition has begun, unevenly, and that retailers organized around channel and functional seams will feel the strain first.

That distinction matters for chief executives. It is easy to overreact to vendor theater and reorganize around a future that is not here. It is just as easy to underreact and assume that today's fragmented operating model can simply be automated into adequacy. The evidence supports neither extreme. It supports a narrower, tougher proposition: AI is making cross-functional coherence more valuable and fragmentation more visible.[6][7][9]

## What the evidence supports, and what it does not

The strongest case for action is organizational, not technological. Across industries, enterprise AI value appears to be constrained less by access to models than by data quality, workflow redesign, governance, and adoption.[1][11][12][14] In retail, legacy architecture, fragmented customer data, and channel-specific systems remain stubborn obstacles to unified decision-making.[13] This is why the most important AI question is often not "Which model?" but "Which outcome, owned by whom, using what shared context and under what policy?"

Call it context architecture: if multiple AI systems are to behave coherently, they need access to consistent customer, product, inventory, and policy context, governed across channels and functions.[13] Without that, intelligence remains isolated. The retailer gets smart fragments rather than a smart enterprise.

The same logic applies to decision seams and rule debt. Problems arise where decisions cross functions, systems, or incentives. Rule debt accumulates when years of exceptions, local policies, and legacy workarounds make automation brittle and inconsistent.[1][13] AI does not remove those problems. It often exposes them.

There is also a fair counterpoint. Not every retailer needs a grand operating-model redesign on day one. The evidence does not support dismissing departmental pilots altogether. Incremental productivity gains are real, and in many firms they are the first sensible source of return.[11][12] A service copilot that reduces routine workload can be worthwhile even if the broader organization remains unchanged. The mistake is to confuse useful augmentation with strategic resolution. Departmental AI can create value. It is unlikely, on its own, to solve cross-functional customer problems.[10][14]

Another complication is that customer-centricity cannot be the only organizing principle. Retailers must also manage margin, inventory productivity, compliance, labor constraints, and brand strategy. A system optimized purely for customer delight can still destroy economics. The more defensible aim is to organize AI around shared customer and commercial outcomes, with explicit governance for trade-offs.[18]

## The CEO's real job

This is where the burden shifts upward. Research on transformation consistently finds that senior leadership matters most when change crosses functional boundaries and requires reallocating decision rights.[1][14][18] In retail, those boundaries are entrenched. Merchandising, marketing, stores, e-commerce, supply chain, and technology often have distinct metrics, budgets, and power centers. No one below the top team can easily redraw the lines.

That does not make the CEO exempt from organizational constraints. The evidence is more sober. Top-level intervention is necessary, not sufficient.[1][14][18] A chief executive can insist that AI be governed around outcomes rather than functions. He or she cannot wish into existence the operating mechanisms that make that real.

Those mechanisms are mundane, which is why they are often neglected. Leaders must define which decisions AI may automate, which it may inform, and which require human judgment.[9][19] Someone must decide how exceptions are escalated, how customer-affecting decisions are observed and audited, and how policy consistency is maintained across channels.[9][19] Someone must reconcile incentives so that one team's AI win is not another team's customer problem.[6][13]

The likely answer is not a wholesale reorganization around AI. The evidence points to something less theatrical and more plausible: a dual operating system. Functional excellence still matters. Retailers need deep expertise in merchandising, supply chain, stores, finance, and brand management. But they also need cross-functional ownership of outcomes such as discovery to conversion, order to resolution, and retention to lifetime value.[18][20] Product operating models and prior waves of digital transformation offer a useful precedent here, even if direct evidence on fully agentic retail operating models remains limited.[14]

This is also the practical meaning of an inverse Conway maneuver. The established principle in software and organizational design is that firms should shape teams and communication structures to produce the architecture they want.[21] Applied to retail AI, the implication is straightforward. If a company wants coherent machine behavior across the customer journey, it must create coherent human governance across the same journey. Otherwise the systems will faithfully reproduce the fragmentation they inherited.

The strategic risk, then, is not simply moving too slowly. It is moving quickly in seven different directions and calling the result transformation. Uncoordinated local initiatives can increase complexity and governance burden even as they generate pockets of short-term gain.[1][14][21]

For retail CEOs, that is the real choice. Not whether to deploy agents, but whether those agents will serve a redesigned operating model or merely automate the old one. The customer will notice the difference before the org chart does.
