> “We shape our buildings; thereafter they shape us.”
> — Winston Churchill, Speech to the House of Commons

**Deck:** The strongest evidence does not show that merchant websites are disappearing, but it does show something important: discovery, evaluation, and even checkout are spreading across search, social, marketplaces, and AI interfaces, forcing retailers to rethink the website as one node in a wider commerce system.

## The website loses its monopoly

For two decades, digital retail strategy was built around a simple assumption: the merchant’s website was the store. Traffic had to be acquired, guided, and converted there. The rest of the internet was a feeder system.

That assumption is weakening. Not collapsing, not yet, but weakening in ways that matter to chief executives. Search engines increasingly answer questions inside the search result itself. Social platforms keep browsing and, in some cases, checkout inside their own apps. Marketplaces have long aggregated demand and much of the customer relationship. Now AI shopping interfaces are beginning to insert themselves into product discovery and comparison as well. The result is not site-free commerce in any full sense. It is commerce that is less site-dependent than many retailers are organized to handle.[[Google AI Overviews in Search](https://blog.google/products/search/generative-ai-search/), Google, 2024; [Meta Shops overview](https://www.facebook.com/business/shops), Meta, accessed 2026; [Shopping in the age of AI: Redefining stores for a new era](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/retail/our-insights/shopping-in-the-age-of-ai-redefining-stores-for-a-new-era), McKinsey & Company, 2024]

Hostinger’s Quick Links feature is a useful prompt for this debate. As described by the company, a seller can create a simple product page and share a payment link through social, messaging, or email. Hostinger has not proved a new retail order. Independent evidence on adoption or strategic impact is thin. The point is that the product captures a real shift in miniature: the transaction can be detached from the full storefront and distributed into other channels.[[Hostinger Horizons: How to use Quick Links](https://support.hostinger.com/en/articles/11837844-hostinger-horizons-how-to-use-quick-links), Hostinger, accessed 2026; [Payment Links](https://stripe.com/payments/payment-links), Stripe, accessed 2026]

That is the defensible thesis. The bolder one, that websites are becoming obsolete, runs ahead of the evidence. Retail sites still matter for brand expression, assortment depth, loyalty, service, and owned data capture. But the website is no longer the only place where product understanding is formed, nor always the place where purchase begins. For many retailers, it is becoming one surface among several.[[Google AI Overviews in Search](https://blog.google/products/search/generative-ai-search/), Google, 2024; [Amazon’s new Rufus shopping assistant](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-rufus-generative-ai-shopping-assistant), About Amazon, 2024]

## Discovery has moved; operations have not

The strategic problem for retailers is not that consumers have stopped visiting websites. It is that the operating model of many retail organizations still assumes they must.

Google’s rollout of AI Overviews illustrates the point clearly. Search is no longer just a referral engine. It is increasingly an answer layer, synthesizing information before a shopper clicks anywhere else. In shopping contexts, that means a customer may form a view of a product, a category, or a shortlist before reaching a merchant-controlled page.[[Google AI Overviews in Search](https://blog.google/products/search/generative-ai-search/), Google, 2024] Social commerce pushes in the same direction. Meta’s Shops infrastructure and TikTok Shop’s seller model are designed to keep browsing and conversion inside their own environments, not to send traffic dutifully back to a retailer’s domain.[[Meta Shops overview](https://www.facebook.com/business/shops), Meta, accessed 2026; [TikTok Shop Academy](https://seller-us.tiktok.com/university/home), TikTok, accessed 2026]

This is already how much of digital commerce works. What is changing is the number of surfaces involved and the growing role of AI in mediating them. Amazon’s Rufus and Google’s AI shopping features suggest that platforms increasingly want to help shoppers narrow options, compare products, and interpret vague intent such as occasion, style, or use case.[[Amazon’s new Rufus shopping assistant](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-rufus-generative-ai-shopping-assistant), About Amazon, 2024; [New ways to shop and get inspired with AI on Google](https://blog.google/products/shopping/new-ways-to-shop-and-get-inspired-with-ai-on-google/), Google, 2024]

The old model treated off-site channels as acquisition and the website as conversion. The emerging model is messier. Discovery may happen in search, social, marketplaces, or conversational interfaces. Evaluation may happen there too. Checkout may happen on a platform, through an accelerated wallet, or via a payment link. The retailer’s own site remains important, but it no longer monopolises persuasion or checkout.[[Shop Pay](https://www.shopify.com/shop-pay), Shopify, accessed 2026; [Payment Links](https://stripe.com/payments/payment-links), Stripe, accessed 2026]

That creates a mismatch. Many retailers still organize digital commerce around homepage performance, site traffic, and on-site conversion. Those metrics remain useful, but they are no longer sufficient if product discovery and decision-making increasingly occur elsewhere. A retailer can become less salient to shoppers before that decline shows up clearly in click-based metrics.

## The real asset is becoming modular

If the website is losing its monopoly, what replaces it as the center of gravity? The evidence points not to a single new storefront, but to a stack of reusable capabilities.

This is why the long-running industry talk about headless and composable commerce deserves a second look. Vendor claims should be treated cautiously. Shopify, Salesforce, and commercetools are not neutral observers. But their product direction is still revealing. All are investing in architectures that expose catalog, pricing, promotions, cart, checkout, and related functions as services rather than binding them tightly to one front end.[[Commerce Components by Shopify](https://www.shopify.com/enterprise/commerce-components), Shopify, accessed 2026; [Commerce Cloud Developer Documentation](https://developer.salesforce.com/docs/commerce), Salesforce, accessed 2026; [Composable Commerce](https://commercetools.com/composable-commerce/), commercetools, accessed 2026]

That matters because distributed commerce is, at heart, an operating problem. A retailer that wants to sell across search, social, marketplaces, messaging, and AI interfaces needs product data that can travel, inventory that can be exposed reliably, pricing and promotions that can be invoked consistently, and checkout that can be embedded or accelerated without breaking trust. In that world, the strategic asset is less the page template than the portability of the underlying commerce functions.[[Standards](https://www.gs1.org/standards), GS1, accessed 2026; [Product data specification](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/7052112), Google Merchant Center Help, accessed 2026]

Product data sits at the center of this shift. Search, recommendation, and conversational systems depend on structured attributes, identifiers, images, availability, and policy information. That has long been true for feeds and marketplaces. AI-mediated discovery raises the stakes because weak data is no longer just an SEO nuisance. It becomes a commercial liability. If a system cannot interpret fit, material, compatibility, delivery promise, or return terms, it cannot represent the product well.[[Standards](https://www.gs1.org/standards), GS1, accessed 2026; [Product data specification](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/7052112), Google Merchant Center Help, accessed 2026]

This is especially awkward in categories such as apparel, where the language of commerce is often imprecise. Size, silhouette, feel, and occasion are hard enough for humans to interpret. They are harder still for machines if the underlying data is sparse or inconsistent. The promise of agentic commerce may be grand. Its prerequisite is mundane: better product information.

## AI may reshape shopping, but the evidence is still early

The temptation is to leap from these shifts to a sweeping conclusion that AI agents will soon shop on consumers’ behalf and relegate merchant websites to the background. That may happen in some categories. The evidence does not yet justify treating it as the base case.

What can be said with confidence is narrower. Major platforms are building shopping assistants and conversational interfaces, and recent industry research suggests consumers are beginning to use generative AI for shopping-related research and inspiration.[[Shopping in the age of AI: Redefining stores for a new era](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/retail/our-insights/shopping-in-the-age-of-ai-redefining-stores-for-a-new-era), McKinsey & Company, 2024; [Amazon’s new Rufus shopping assistant](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-rufus-generative-ai-shopping-assistant), About Amazon, 2024] That is enough to justify executive attention. It is not enough to prove that autonomous purchasing agents will become a mainstream retail channel soon.

The strongest academic evidence in the brief comes from a different but related domain: customer support. A field study by Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond found that generative AI improved productivity in customer-service work, with especially large gains for less experienced workers.[[Generative AI at Work](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161), National Bureau of Economic Research, 2023] This does not prove that AI will transform shopping behavior. It does suggest that AI can codify product knowledge and service guidance in ways that make conversational selling and support more scalable.

That distinction matters. AI is already credible as an operating tool. It is plausible, but less proven, as a new demand channel. Retail leaders should not confuse the two.

The same caution applies to distributed checkout. Payment links, accelerated wallets, and embedded checkout flows are established tools. Stripe and Shopify both support models that let merchants transact without requiring a full browsing session on a traditional storefront.[[Payment Links](https://stripe.com/payments/payment-links), Stripe, accessed 2026; [Shop Pay](https://www.shopify.com/shop-pay), Shopify, accessed 2026] Hostinger’s twist appears to be compressing product-page creation and transaction setup into a lightweight workflow. That is operationally interesting, especially for small sellers or simple catalogs. It is not yet evidence that large retailers with complex assortments, loyalty programs, and omnichannel fulfillment can or should replace the fuller storefront experience.[[Hostinger Horizons: How to use Quick Links](https://support.hostinger.com/en/articles/11837844-hostinger-horizons-how-to-use-quick-links), Hostinger, accessed 2026]

The likely near-term outcome is more prosaic and more important. Product content will become machine-first as well as human-first. Retailers will need to assume that search engines, marketplaces, recommendation systems, and AI assistants are all reading the catalog, not just the customer. That is less glamorous than the idea of autonomous agents. It is also much better supported.[[Product data specification](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/7052112), Google Merchant Center Help, accessed 2026; [Standards](https://www.gs1.org/standards), GS1, accessed 2026]

## Distribution brings reach, and dependence

There is, however, a serious complication in the cheerful story that merchants can simply sell wherever the customer begins. Distribution increases reach. It can also increase dependence.

Retail history offers a warning. When commerce shifts into third-party environments, the platform often captures more than convenience. It captures data asymmetry, fee leverage, and control over the customer interface. Marketplaces have shown this for years. AI interfaces could repeat the pattern in a new form. A retailer may become more discoverable across surfaces while becoming less visible as a brand and less powerful as a merchant.

This is where the original “site-free commerce” thesis needs trimming. The question is not only whether transactions can be detached from the store. They can. The harder question is who owns the control point once they are detached.

For some categories, the answer may favor platforms. Commodity goods, replenishment items, and tightly specified purchases may be easier to compare and easier to route through external agents or universal checkout layers. For other categories, especially premium fashion, luxury, and high-consideration purchases, the merchant site still does work that distributed interfaces struggle to replicate. Editorial context, visual identity, service reassurance, and emotional differentiation are not incidental extras. They are part of the product.[[Shopping in the age of AI: Redefining stores for a new era](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/retail/our-insights/shopping-in-the-age-of-ai-redefining-stores-for-a-new-era), McKinsey & Company, 2024]

Trust is the other constraint. Distributed commerce only works if trust signals travel with it. Delivery promises, returns policies, authenticity guarantees, and review quality must be legible outside the merchant’s own environment. A well-built site can signal trust. A machine-mediated channel has to state it clearly and consistently. That is one reason the website is unlikely to disappear. It remains a place where the retailer can explain itself fully, even if it is no longer the only place where selling happens.

There is also a governance problem. If retailers use AI to generate product descriptions, recommendations, or customer-facing advice, they inherit risks around accuracy, consistency, and accountability. The [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) is not retail-specific, but it is a useful reminder that customer-facing AI is not just a productivity tool. It is a source of operational and reputational risk if left unmanaged.[[AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework), NIST, 2023]

## Where the control points move next

The practical implication for chief executives is not to abandon the website. It is to stop treating it as the sole container of commerce capability.

The retailers best placed for this shift will not necessarily be those with the best-designed storefronts or the noisiest AI announcements. They will be those whose product data is clean enough for machines to interpret, whose commerce services can be exposed across channels, whose trust signals travel well, and whose organizations can govern all this without descending into channel conflict.[[Standards](https://www.gs1.org/standards), GS1, accessed 2026; [Commerce Components by Shopify](https://www.shopify.com/enterprise/commerce-components), Shopify, accessed 2026; [AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework), NIST, 2023]

That shifts the executive conversation. The old question was how to drive more shoppers to the site. The better one now is whether a customer who never visits the site can still discover, understand, trust, and buy the product accurately. If the answer is no, the retailer is still organized for a world in which the website is the store. The evidence suggests that world is fading, even if it is not gone.[[Google AI Overviews in Search](https://blog.google/products/search/generative-ai-search/), Google, 2024; [Meta Shops overview](https://www.facebook.com/business/shops), Meta, accessed 2026; [Amazon’s new Rufus shopping assistant](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-rufus-generative-ai-shopping-assistant), About Amazon, 2024]

What to watch next is not whether one startup or platform declares the end of the storefront. It is whether the control points of retail keep moving outward: from homepage to feed, from product page to answer layer, from branded checkout to portable transaction service, and perhaps, in time, from shopper to software. The first three shifts are already visible. The fourth remains plausible, but unproven.
